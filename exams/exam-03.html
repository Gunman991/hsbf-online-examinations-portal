CASE STUDY: THE SILENT BREACH AT MERIDIAN HEALTH AND FINANCIAL SERVICES
Meridian Health and Financial Services (MHFS) is a privately owned organisation that provides medical insurance, financial advisory services, and employee benefits management. It employs 180 staff members and serves approximately 12,000 clients.
The organisation stores sensitive information, including client names, national identification numbers, medical histories, insurance claims, bank account details, salary records, passwords, and copies of identity documents.
MHFS recently introduced a cloud-based customer management system to improve efficiency. However, the implementation was rushed because management wanted the system operational before the end of the financial year. The IT department consisted of only four employees, none of whom had received formal data protection training during the previous two years.
Three weeks after the new system was introduced, a junior accounts officer received an email that appeared to originate from the organisation’s finance director. The email instructed the officer to open an attached document and verify an urgent supplier payment. The message used the finance director’s correct name, professional signature, and a convincing explanation of why the payment was urgent.
The attachment prompted the officer to sign in using their corporate credentials. Unknown to the officer, the website was fraudulent. Within minutes, an unauthorised person used the stolen credentials to access the organisation’s cloud system.
During the following five days, the unauthorised person accessed several client records and downloaded a substantial amount of information. The attacker also created a new administrator account using a display name similar to that of the legitimate IT manager. Because the organisation had not enabled multi-factor authentication for all accounts, the attacker was able to continue accessing the system.
The breach was discovered when a client complained that someone had contacted them using details from a confidential medical insurance claim. An IT technician subsequently found unusual login times, unfamiliar IP addresses, and evidence of large data transfers.
The investigation revealed several additional weaknesses:
Several employees shared accounts when working on urgent assignments.
Former employees’ accounts had not been disabled promptly.
Sensitive files were stored in a shared cloud folder accessible to more staff than necessary.
Backups existed, but their restoration had never been tested.
The organisation had no formal incident response plan.
Management had assumed that the cloud provider was entirely responsible for cybersecurity.
There was no clear procedure for determining whether a data breach had to be reported to a regulator or communicated to affected individuals.
Some employees suggested deleting suspicious logs to prevent reputational damage.
The finance director wanted the organisation to continue normal operations without informing clients until the investigation was complete.
The organisation had never conducted a comprehensive data protection risk assessment.
The board has appointed an independent cybersecurity and data protection team to investigate the incident, determine the organisation’s failures, and recommend measures to prevent a recurrence.
You are a member of the appointed team. Answer all four questions using the facts, uncertainties, and competing interests presented in this case.
QUESTION 1: BREACH INVESTIGATION AND CRITICAL INCIDENT ANALYSIS
[25 MARKS]
The board believes that the incident was caused entirely by the junior accounts officer’s failure to recognise a phishing email. The officer, however, argues that the organisation’s poor security controls made the attack possible.
As an investigator, you must determine whether either explanation is sufficient.
Required:
a) Critically analyse the sequence of events and distinguish between the initial attack method, the vulnerabilities exploited, the attacker’s actions, and the evidence indicating that a breach occurred. (7 marks)
b) Evaluate the responsibility of the employee, the IT department, senior management, and the cloud service provider. Explain why assigning blame to one individual may provide an incomplete explanation of the incident. (6 marks)
c) Identify and prioritise five pieces of digital evidence that should be collected to establish the extent and cause of the breach. Explain what each piece of evidence could reveal and how its integrity should be preserved. (7 marks)
d) Explain how investigators should respond to the suggestion that suspicious logs be deleted to protect the organisation’s reputation. Discuss the consequences of destroying or altering potentially relevant evidence. (5 marks)
QUESTION 2: DATA PROTECTION, PRIVACY AND LEGAL RESPONSIBILITY
[25 MARKS]
The investigation confirms that unauthorised access occurred and that client information was downloaded. However, the team has not yet established exactly which individuals’ records were accessed, whether every downloaded file was opened, or whether the attacker has shared the information with other parties.
The finance director argues that the organisation should not notify clients because doing so could cause panic, loss of business, and reputational damage. Another manager claims that because the information was stored in the cloud, responsibility rests solely with the cloud provider.
Required:
a) Analyse the types of information involved in the incident and explain why the exposure of medical records, identity documents, financial details, and authentication credentials may create different levels and forms of harm. (6 marks)
b) Evaluate the organisation’s responsibilities for protecting personal information throughout its collection, storage, access, use, disclosure, and disposal. Explain why outsourcing data storage does not automatically eliminate the organisation’s responsibilities. (6 marks)
c) Critically assess the finance director’s decision to delay notifying affected individuals. Explain what factors should determine whether notification is necessary, when it should occur, and what information should be communicated. Consider the applicable legal requirements rather than assuming that every breach follows the same notification rule. (7 marks)
d) Recommend how the organisation should determine its legal and regulatory obligations. In your answer, consider applicable data protection legislation, contractual obligations, evidence of harm, the possibility of further misuse, and the need for accurate documentation. (6 marks)
QUESTION 3: SECURITY CONTROL FAILURES AND RISK MANAGEMENT
[25 MARKS]
The board has instructed the IT department to recommend security improvements. The department proposes purchasing an expensive security monitoring platform immediately. However, one board member argues that the organisation should first address basic weaknesses in account management, staff awareness, access permissions, and incident preparedness.
The organisation has a limited cybersecurity budget and cannot implement every proposed control at once.
Required:
a) Analyse at least five weaknesses identified in the scenario. For each weakness, explain how it contributed to the incident or increased the potential impact. (7 marks)
b) Develop a prioritised risk treatment plan containing at least five practical security controls. Justify the order in which the controls should be implemented, considering likelihood, potential impact, cost, and urgency. (7 marks)
c) Evaluate the strengths and limitations of technical controls, administrative controls, and employee awareness training. Explain why relying exclusively on any one of these categories would leave the organisation exposed. (5 marks)
d) Explain how the organisation could apply least privilege, multi-factor authentication, secure account lifecycle management, encryption, tested backups, and continuous monitoring to reduce the likelihood or impact of a similar incident. Identify which risks each measure would address and which risks would remain. (6 marks)
QUESTION 4: INCIDENT RESPONSE, ETHICAL DECISION-MAKING AND BUSINESS CONTINUITY
[25 MARKS]
The board must decide how to manage the aftermath of the breach. Some executives want all systems shut down immediately. Others fear that shutting down the cloud platform will interrupt medical insurance approvals and delay payments to clients. The IT department warns that continued access may allow the attacker to remain active.
Meanwhile, clients are requesting explanations, employees are sharing unverified information on social media, and the organisation’s external communications team wants to issue a statement before the investigation has established the full extent of the breach.
Required:
a) Design a phased incident response plan covering the initial response, containment, evidence preservation, eradication, recovery, and post-incident review. Justify the actions that should occur at each stage. (8 marks)
b) Evaluate the competing risks of shutting down the affected systems immediately versus maintaining operations during the investigation. Recommend a decision-making approach that balances evidence preservation, security, patient or client welfare, and business continuity. (6 marks)
c) Develop a communication strategy for affected clients, employees, regulators where applicable, and other relevant stakeholders. Explain how the organisation can communicate honestly without disclosing unverified conclusions or unnecessarily exposing additional sensitive information. (5 marks)
d) Recommend four long-term governance measures that would help prevent a recurrence. Explain how senior management should measure whether the improvements are effective rather than simply confirming that new policies have been written. (6 marks)
END OF EXAMINATION

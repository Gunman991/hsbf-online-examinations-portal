Instructions to Candidates
Answer all four questions. Each question carries 25 marks.
All questions relate to the single integrated scenario presented below.
Responses may be written as structured essays or well-developed points, provided that the analysis is coherent, rigorous and adequately justified.
Mere identification of legal concepts, reproduction of definitions or unsupported conclusions will not earn high marks.
Critically evaluate competing explanations, evidential limitations, legal risks, professional duties and the consequences of alternative investigative decisions.
Distinguish carefully between suspicion, reasonable inference, an investigative conclusion and a determination of guilt by a competent court.
Apply Zimbabwean law where relevant, identifying legal uncertainties and the need to verify applicable legislation and procedural requirements. Do not invent statutory provisions or assume that principles from another jurisdiction apply automatically.
Where the scenario does not provide enough information to reach a conclusion, identify the missing evidence and explain how it should be obtained lawfully.
MASTER SCENARIO: THE MAVUNO FINANCIAL CONSORTIUM FRAUD INVESTIGATION
Mavuno Financial Consortium (MFC) is a fictional financial services group headquartered in Harare, Zimbabwe. It operates a microfinance institution, a digital payments platform and a procurement subsidiary. The group manages funds belonging to private investors, corporate clients and individual customers.
In March 2026, an external audit identifies unexplained payments totalling approximately US$2.8 million over 18 months. The payments were processed through several suppliers, two offshore payment intermediaries and a recently established local consultancy. Some transactions were authorised through MFC’s ordinary approval system, while others were processed using senior employees’ credentials outside normal working hours.
The board appoints an independent fraud examination team led by Dr. N., a certified fraud examiner with digital forensic experience. The board’s mandate requires the team to determine the nature and extent of the suspected financial misconduct, identify control failures, preserve relevant evidence and prepare findings that may be used in disciplinary proceedings, civil litigation or a criminal investigation.
During the investigation, the team discovers the following circumstances.
Incident 1: The disputed search
The chief executive provides Dr. N. with a laptop belonging to the finance director, claiming that the device contains evidence of fraudulent payments. The chief executive states that the finance director has been suspended and that the company therefore has unrestricted authority to inspect the device.
The laptop is protected by encryption and contains both corporate records and personal communications. An IT administrator bypasses the login controls using credentials obtained from a colleague. Before a forensic image is created, the administrator opens several folders, copies selected documents and deletes files described as irrelevant.
The finance director subsequently alleges that the device contained privileged communications with legal advisers and personal information unrelated to the investigation. The director also challenges the authority under which the device was accessed.
Incident 2: The compromised evidence trail
A forensic specialist later creates a forensic image of the laptop. A cryptographic hash is recorded, but no hash was calculated before the administrator’s initial access. The team cannot establish exactly which files were altered, deleted or copied before imaging.
The USB drive recovered from the finance director’s office is initially stored in an unlocked drawer. Two investigators handle it without recording the transfers. One investigator later admits connecting it to a personal computer to inspect its contents.
Meanwhile, a cloud-service provider supplies transaction logs following a request from MFC’s legal department. The records show successful logins from different geographical locations, but the service provider cannot initially confirm whether the locations represent actual users, virtual private networks or automated services.
The prosecution later considers relying on the digital records to establish who authorised the payments.
Incident 3: The pressured confession
The procurement director, Mr. K., is interviewed after several suspicious supplier payments are traced to a company owned by his sibling. The interview takes place in a boardroom, with the chief executive present for part of the questioning.
Mr. K. is told that he will be dismissed and publicly identified as a criminal unless he cooperates. The chief executive threatens to report his spouse to law enforcement unless he signs a statement accepting responsibility for the transactions.
Mr. K. signs a statement admitting that he approved the payments. He later retracts the statement, alleging intimidation, exhaustion and misleading questioning. He maintains that he approved some payments in good faith and that other transactions were processed using his credentials without his knowledge.
The interview was audio-recorded, but the recording has several gaps. The written transcript does not indicate all interruptions or periods when the recorder was switched off.
Incident 4: Conflicting financial evidence
The investigation identifies three possible explanations for the payments:
Hypothesis A: Mr. K. deliberately approved fraudulent payments in exchange for undisclosed personal benefits.
Hypothesis B: A group of employees exploited weaknesses in the payment system and used Mr. K.’s credentials without his knowledge.
Hypothesis C: Some payments were legitimate commercial transactions, while others resulted from poor controls, conflicts of interest or negligence rather than deliberate fraud.
A bank statement shows that a company linked to Mr. K.’s sibling received substantial payments from MFC. However, the investigators have not yet established the ultimate beneficial ownership of all intermediaries or traced the full destination of the money.
An email refers to a payment as a “special arrangement,” but the sender argues that the phrase referred to a legitimate commercial discount. Several invoices support the delivery of goods, although the quantities and prices do not consistently match the procurement records.
A whistleblower claims that senior executives knew about the payment arrangements. The whistleblower provides screenshots but refuses to disclose the source of the original messages, citing fear of retaliation.
Incident 5: Institutional pressure and proposed testimony
Before the final report is completed, the board instructs Dr. N. to identify Mr. K. as the principal architect of the fraud. The board argues that the institution cannot tolerate uncertainty and that investors require a decisive conclusion.
The board also requests that Dr. N. exclude the legitimate invoices, omit the disputed evidence-handling procedures and describe the confession as voluntary. A board member privately suggests that the consultancy’s future contracts depend on producing a report that supports the board’s position.
Dr. N. discovers that one member of the investigative team previously provided paid consultancy services to a company linked to one of the payment intermediaries. This relationship was not disclosed when the team was appointed.
The matter is referred to legal advisers, who indicate that civil recovery proceedings and a possible criminal prosecution may follow. Dr. N. is expected to provide expert evidence and may face rigorous cross-examination regarding investigative independence, evidential integrity, legal authority, alternative explanations and the scientific basis of the conclusions.
Assume that you have been appointed as an independent doctoral-level adviser reviewing the investigation’s legal defensibility, evidential reliability and suitability for court proceedings.
QUESTION 1: LEGAL AUTHORITY, FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS AND INVESTIGATIVE LEGITIMACY
25 marks
Critically evaluate whether the investigation, from its inception to the acquisition of the laptop, USB drive and cloud records, can produce legally defensible evidence.
Your answer must address the following analytical issues:
The distinction between an employer’s legitimate authority to investigate corporate misconduct and the lawful authority required to access protected devices, private communications and potentially privileged material.
The legal and ethical implications of bypassing access controls, copying documents and deleting files before forensic preservation.
The relevance of constitutional protections, privacy, lawful search and seizure, confidentiality and procedural fairness under applicable Zimbabwean law.
Whether a defect in the manner evidence was obtained necessarily makes that evidence inadmissible, unreliable or unusable for every purpose. Distinguish these questions rather than treating them as interchangeable.
The potential consequences of the investigative team’s conduct for civil proceedings, disciplinary action and a criminal prosecution.
A defensible legal and investigative framework for correcting procedural deficiencies, preserving remaining evidence and documenting decisions without retrospectively disguising irregularities.
Required standard: Develop a reasoned legal argument that considers competing interpretations, identifies facts requiring further verification and reaches a justified conclusion. Do not simply list legal principles.
QUESTION 2: DIGITAL FORENSICS, EVIDENTIAL INTEGRITY AND COMPETING HYPOTHESES
25 marks
The prosecution proposes to rely on the laptop image, USB contents, cloud logs, transaction records, emails and whistleblower screenshots to establish that Mr. K. deliberately participated in fraud.
Construct a critical evidential assessment of the proposed case.
Your answer must:
Evaluate the consequences of the missing pre-acquisition hash, undocumented handling, possible file modification and uncertain provenance of the digital records.
Distinguish integrity, authenticity, attribution, reliability, admissibility and evidential weight. Explain why establishing one does not automatically establish the others.
Assess the extent to which a login record, electronic approval, bank transfer, suspicious email or financial connection can establish the identity, knowledge and intention of a particular individual.
Compare Hypotheses A, B and C. Identify evidence that would support or weaken each explanation and explain how investigators should avoid confirmation bias.
Design a proportionate, defensible evidence-verification plan, including preservation of originals, forensic imaging where appropriate, validation of hashes, audit-log analysis, corroboration of financial transactions and examination of potentially exculpatory material.
Explain how the investigation should record uncertainty, conflicting evidence, methodological limitations and any conclusions that cannot reliably be reached.
Required standard: Your analysis must demonstrate advanced reasoning about evidential inference and investigative methodology. A technically correct procedure is insufficient unless you explain what the procedure can and cannot prove.
QUESTION 3: INTERVIEW LEGALITY, CONFESSIONS AND PROFESSIONAL ETHICS
25 marks
Critically examine the evidential and ethical status of Mr. K.’s signed statement and the conduct of the interview.
Your response must analyse:
The implications of threats against Mr. K. and his spouse, possible intimidation, exhaustion, leading questions and interruptions to the recording.
The distinction between a signed statement, a voluntary admission, a reliable confession and proof of the alleged offence.
The relevance of applicable constitutional protections, criminal procedure, evidentiary rules and the legal distinction between an internal workplace interview and questioning conducted by authorised law-enforcement officials.
The appropriate response to a retracted statement, including preservation of the original recording, independent examination of the transcript, documentation of the allegation and lawful corroboration of the material assertions.
How investigators should test Mr. K.’s claim that some payments were legitimate and others were processed using his credentials without his knowledge.
The professional obligations of a fraud examiner when an employer demands a predetermined conclusion, particularly where the examiner’s continued employment or future contracts may be affected.
The safeguards necessary to protect the credibility of the investigation and the fairness of any subsequent proceedings.
Required standard: Reach a reasoned conclusion about what use, if any, can safely be made of the statement. Identify the additional facts and legal determinations needed before deciding its admissibility or evidential weight. Do not assume that either the retraction or the original signature settles the issue.
QUESTION 4: EXPERT TESTIMONY, DISCLOSURE AND THE DEFENSIBILITY OF THE FINAL REPORT
25 marks
Assume that the matter proceeds to court and Dr. N. is called to testify as an expert witness. The opposing lawyer challenges the investigator’s independence, qualifications, handling of evidence, methodology and conclusions. The lawyer also establishes that the board requested the exclusion of potentially exculpatory material.
Prepare a comprehensive critical analysis explaining how Dr. N. should respond and how the investigation should be made fit for legal scrutiny.
Your answer must:
Distinguish the roles of a fact witness, an expert witness, an investigator and the court. Explain the limits of the examiner’s authority to express opinions about guilt.
Evaluate the undisclosed consultancy relationship and the board’s attempt to influence the report. Explain how actual or perceived conflicts of interest affect credibility and the weight given to expert evidence.
Explain the duties of objectivity, independence, accuracy, transparency and appropriate disclosure of material that may contradict the preferred conclusion, subject to applicable law and lawful disclosure obligations.
Design a defensible structure for the final report, including the mandate, methodology, evidence sources, chain-of-custody records, findings, alternative hypotheses, limitations, conclusions and supporting documentation.
Develop a strategy for answering difficult cross-examination questions without exaggerating certainty, speculating beyond the evidence or concealing errors.
Evaluate the consequences of misleading testimony, selective reporting, unsupported attribution or deliberate suppression of relevant evidence for the examiner, the proceedings and the institution.
Recommend whether Dr. N. should continue leading the investigation, disclose the conflict, obtain independent review or withdraw from a particular role. Justify the recommendation using the facts and applicable professional obligations.
Required standard: Your answer must integrate legal analysis, evidential reasoning, expert-witness ethics and practical courtroom strategy. A recommendation is not sufficient without examining its implications and defending it against credible alternatives.
END OF EXAMINATION

CASE STUDY: THE MISSING MILLIONS AT CRESTPOINT MICROFINANCE
Crest Point Microfinance is a financial services company that provides loans, processes customer repayments, and manages electronic transactions for individuals and small businesses. The company employs 95 staff members and maintains a central computer server, a finance department network, employee workstations, and a cloud-based transaction management platform.
For several months, the company’s management had noticed discrepancies between customer repayment records and the amounts appearing in its financial reports. Some customers claimed that they had repaid their loans in full, yet the system continued to show outstanding balances. In other cases, transactions appeared in the system without corresponding bank deposits.
The finance manager initially attributed the discrepancies to accounting errors. However, an internal review identified several transactions that appeared to have been altered after their original recording.
The investigation became more serious when a junior finance officer reported receiving instructions from a senior manager to modify certain transaction records. The officer claimed that the instructions had been sent through email and a workplace messaging application.
Two days later, the company discovered that several transaction records and email messages were missing from the finance officer’s workstation. The workstation had also been used to access the central transaction management platform shortly before the discrepancies were discovered.
The company suspected that the finance officer might have participated in the fraudulent transactions. However, the officer denied wrongdoing and alleged that another employee had remotely accessed the workstation using shared credentials.
The company’s IT department immediately disconnected the workstation from the office network, shut it down, and placed it in a locked office. No photographs were taken before the workstation was moved, and no written record was made of who handled it. The workstation had not been forensically imaged, and its original storage drive remained installed.
During the initial review, the IT technician used ordinary file browsing to search for deleted documents. The technician also opened several suspicious files and copied them to a USB drive. The technician could not explain whether the copied files were identical to the originals or whether the actions had changed any metadata.
A subsequent inspection revealed that:
The workstation contained a standard hard disk drive and had been running a Windows operating system.
The employee’s account had access to confidential financial records and customer information.
Several files had modification timestamps that appeared inconsistent with the dates on which the transactions were allegedly processed.
The workstation contained browser history suggesting access to the company’s transaction platform outside normal working hours.
A USB storage device had recently been connected to the workstation.
Some relevant emails were missing from the local mailbox, although copies might still exist on the company’s email server.
The organisation had centralised login records, but the retention period was limited.
The server’s transaction logs showed changes to several financial records, but the organisation had not yet established which user or device had initiated every change.
The workstation’s clock might not have been synchronised with the server’s clock.
The company had no formal digital evidence handling procedure and had never trained its staff in computer forensic investigations.
Management instructed an independent forensic investigation team to determine what happened, identify the relevant evidence, establish whether the workstation had been used to manipulate financial records, and prepare findings suitable for disciplinary or legal proceedings if warranted.
You are a member of the appointed computer forensic investigation team. Answer all four questions using the case study as your primary source of evidence.
QUESTION 1: DIGITAL EVIDENCE IDENTIFICATION, PRESERVATION AND CHAIN OF CUSTODY
[25 MARKS]
The company’s IT technician believes that the workstation is ready for examination because it has already been disconnected from the network and placed in a locked office. The investigation supervisor, however, is concerned that the initial handling may have compromised the integrity of the evidence.
As the forensic investigator, you must determine how the investigation should proceed.
Required:
a) Identify and analyse at least five potential sources of digital evidence in the case. Explain what each source might reveal about the suspected manipulation of financial records. (6 marks)
b) Critically evaluate the IT technician’s decision to shut down the workstation, browse its files, open suspicious documents, and copy files to a USB device before forensic acquisition. Explain the possible consequences for evidence integrity, metadata, and volatile information. (6 marks)
c) Develop a step-by-step procedure for securing, documenting, acquiring, and preserving the workstation and other relevant digital evidence. Include appropriate considerations for photographs, device identification, storage media, forensic imaging, hashing, and chain of custody. (8 marks)
d) Explain how an investigator should respond if a relevant file is discovered to have been modified during the initial IT inspection. Discuss how the team can document the limitation, assess the remaining evidence, and maintain investigative credibility. (5 marks)
QUESTION 2: FORENSIC TOOLS, ACQUISITION AND TECHNICAL EXAMINATION
[25 MARKS]
The investigation team has obtained approval to conduct a forensic examination of the workstation, its storage drive, the USB device, and relevant server records.
The team must select suitable tools and techniques without unnecessarily altering the original evidence. The investigation may eventually be reviewed by management, external auditors, legal representatives, or a court.
Required:
a) Recommend appropriate forensic tools for the acquisition and examination of the workstation and associated evidence. Discuss the roles and limitations of tools such as FTK Imager, Autopsy, EnCase, Magnet AXIOM, and relevant operating-system or log-analysis utilities. Explain why a tool should be selected according to the investigative requirement rather than its popularity. (7 marks)
b) Explain how a forensic image should be acquired and verified. Discuss the purpose of a write blocker where appropriate, cryptographic hashing, verification of the acquired image, and preservation of the original storage device. (6 marks)
c) Describe how the investigation team could use forensic examination techniques to investigate deleted files, file metadata, browser history, USB connection artefacts, email records, and relevant transaction logs. Explain what each technique could establish and what it could not conclusively prove. (7 marks)
d) The team finds that a file’s modification timestamp differs from the timestamp recorded by the transaction server. Evaluate at least three possible explanations for this discrepancy and explain how investigators should correlate timestamps before drawing conclusions. (5 marks)
QUESTION 3: DIGITAL ARTEFACT ANALYSIS, TIMELINE RECONSTRUCTION AND ATTRIBUTION
[25 MARKS]
The preliminary forensic examination identifies the following findings:
A document containing transaction details was deleted from the workstation.
A USB storage device was connected shortly before the deletion.
The browser history indicates access to the transaction management platform after normal working hours.
Server logs record changes to several customer repayment records.
An email relating to the changes is missing from the local mailbox.
The workstation’s user account was used to access the system, but the organisation cannot establish whether the account holder was physically present.
Some timestamps differ between the workstation and the server.
No direct evidence has yet established who physically performed the changes or whether the finance officer acted with fraudulent intent.
Management wants the investigation team to identify the responsible employee as quickly as possible.
Required:
a) Explain how investigators could correlate file system artefacts, browser history, USB artefacts, email evidence, user account activity, and server logs to construct a defensible timeline of events. Identify the significance and limitations of each source. (7 marks)
b) Analyse the distinction between establishing that an action occurred on a device, establishing that a particular user account was involved, and establishing that a particular individual performed the action. Explain why these conclusions are not necessarily equivalent. (6 marks)
c) Propose a method for investigating the deleted transaction document and missing email. Explain how deleted-file recovery, email server records, backups, metadata, and other corroborating evidence could support or challenge the competing explanations. (6 marks)
d) Evaluate whether the preliminary findings are sufficient to conclude that the finance officer committed fraud. Identify at least three alternative explanations or uncertainties that must be investigated and explain what additional evidence could help resolve them. (6 marks)
QUESTION 4: FORENSIC REPORTING, PROFESSIONAL ETHICS AND EVIDENCE PRESENTATION
[25 MARKS]
After two weeks of investigation, the forensic team has recovered some deleted documents, verified forensic images, analysed selected server logs, and developed a preliminary timeline. However, certain emails remain unavailable, some logs have expired, and the initial IT handling of the workstation has created uncertainties about the original state of some files.
The managing director demands a report stating conclusively that the finance officer committed fraud. The finance manager also asks the team to exclude evidence that might suggest another employee had access to the workstation.
The investigation supervisor refuses to make a conclusion that is not supported by the evidence.
Required:
a) Explain how the forensic team should structure a professional forensic report. Identify the essential sections and describe how the team should distinguish observations, examination methods, analytical interpretations, limitations, and conclusions. (6 marks)
b) Critically evaluate the ethical and professional implications of management’s request to predetermine the conclusion and exclude potentially relevant evidence. Explain how impartiality, objectivity, confidentiality, and professional accountability should guide the investigation. (6 marks)
c) Describe how the team should present its findings to a non-technical audience while retaining sufficient technical detail for independent review. Explain how exhibits, hash values, evidence references, timelines, screenshots, and documented procedures can support the report. (6 marks)
d) Recommend practical improvements to the organisation’s digital evidence handling and incident investigation procedures. Include staff training, evidence access controls, chain-of-custody documentation, acquisition and verification procedures, log retention, and periodic testing of the investigation process. (7 marks)
END OF EXAMINATION

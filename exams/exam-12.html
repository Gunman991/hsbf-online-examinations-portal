CASE STUDY: THE DIGITAL TRANSFORMATION FAILURE AT NOVACREST DISTRIBUTION GROUP
NovaCrest Distribution Group (NDG) is a medium-sized company that distributes electronic equipment, office supplies, and household products to retailers across several cities. The organisation employs 240 staff members across its head office, warehouse, finance department, sales department, and delivery operations.
For several years, NDG relied on paper records, spreadsheets, telephone calls, and standalone computer systems to manage its daily activities. Customer information was maintained by the sales department, stock records were updated manually by warehouse employees, and the finance department used a separate accounting application to manage invoices and payments.
Management frequently complained about inaccurate stock figures, delayed financial reports, duplicated customer records, and disagreements between departments. In some instances, the company accepted orders for products that were no longer available. In others, customers received invoices containing incorrect prices.
To address these problems, the managing director approved the introduction of an integrated Information System that would combine inventory management, customer relationship management, accounting, human resources, and sales reporting.
The project was assigned to the IT department, which consisted of three technicians. Management expected the system to be operational within six weeks and instructed the team to minimise expenditure on training and system testing.
The new system was implemented with the following weaknesses:
The inventory, finance, and sales departments continued using some of their old spreadsheets alongside the new system.
Different departments used inconsistent product codes and customer identification formats.
Several employees were given administrator privileges because management wanted to avoid delays when staff needed assistance.
The company network used a single shared password for several warehouse workstations.
Some computers were running outdated operating systems and had not received recent security updates.
Employees had not received formal training on password security, phishing, access permissions, or the handling of confidential information.
The new system was connected to the internet for remote access, but multi-factor authentication was not enabled for all users.
Management assumed that purchasing antivirus software was sufficient to protect the organisation from cyberattacks.
Backups were configured, but no one had tested whether the company could restore its information after a major failure.
There was no documented procedure for reporting security incidents or responding to suspected cyberattacks.
Two months after implementation, a major incident occurred.
The sales department recorded a large customer order in the new system. The warehouse department, however, continued relying on an outdated spreadsheet that showed a different stock balance. Employees dispatched products that had already been allocated to another customer.
At approximately the same time, the finance department discovered several invoices containing altered bank account details. One supplier claimed that a payment had been diverted to an unauthorised account. Investigators later found that an employee had received a convincing email requesting that supplier payment details be updated urgently.
The IT department also discovered unusual login activity on an employee account outside normal working hours. The account had access to customer records, financial information, and parts of the inventory system. It was not immediately clear whether the account had been compromised or whether the activity was legitimate remote work.
As management investigated these incidents, additional problems emerged. Some customer records were duplicated, certain transactions had incomplete audit trails, and the IT department could not confidently establish which version of the inventory data was accurate.
The managing director blamed the new Information System and demanded that it be removed immediately. The finance manager argued that the problem was caused by employees who failed to follow procedures. The IT team maintained that the system had been introduced without sufficient planning, training, security controls, or management support.
The board has appointed an independent Information Systems and Cyber Security Foundations assessment team to determine the underlying causes, evaluate the risks, and recommend a sustainable solution.
You are a member of this assessment team. Answer all four questions using the scenario as your primary source of evidence.
QUESTION 1: INFORMATION SYSTEMS, BUSINESS PROCESSES AND DECISION-MAKING
[25 MARKS]
The managing director argues that the newly introduced Information System has failed and should be removed. Other managers believe that the organisation’s problems existed before the system was implemented and have simply become more visible.
As an Information Systems analyst, you must evaluate these competing explanations.
Required:
a) Analyse the role of an Information System within NDG. Explain how people, processes, data, hardware, software, and networks should work together to support the organisation’s operations and objectives. (6 marks)
b) Critically evaluate how the continued use of old spreadsheets alongside the new system contributed to operational errors. Explain why purchasing an integrated system does not automatically guarantee accurate information or improved decision-making. (6 marks)
c) Identify and analyse four underlying causes of the organisation’s problems. Distinguish between technological failures, human factors, management failures, and weaknesses in business processes. (7 marks)
d) Recommend whether NDG should immediately abandon the new system, continue using it without changes, or retain and improve it through a controlled process. Justify your recommendation using business continuity, cost, reliability, information quality, and operational risk. (6 marks)
QUESTION 2: COMPUTER SYSTEMS, NETWORKS AND CYBER SECURITY FOUNDATIONS
[25 MARKS]
The IT department confirms that the company uses connected computers, shared warehouse workstations, internet-based remote access, antivirus software, and a central Information System. However, management cannot explain how these components interact or why the existing security measures have not prevented the incidents.
The board requires a technical explanation that can guide future security decisions.
Required:
a) Explain how hardware, operating systems, application software, databases, and computer networks interact to support NDG’s Information System. Analyse how a failure in one component could affect other components and disrupt business operations. (6 marks)
b) Analyse the possible relationship between the suspicious employee login, the altered supplier bank details, the shared workstation password, outdated operating systems, and excessive administrator privileges. Distinguish between confirmed facts and reasonable hypotheses that require further investigation. (7 marks)
c) Evaluate the effectiveness and limitations of antivirus software as a security measure. Recommend additional technical and administrative controls that would address the weaknesses identified in the scenario. (6 marks)
d) Explain how the principles of confidentiality, integrity, and availability apply to NDG. Use specific examples from the case to demonstrate how each principle has been threatened and identify an appropriate control for each threat. (6 marks)
QUESTION 3: DATA MANAGEMENT, INFORMATION QUALITY AND SYSTEM SECURITY
[25 MARKS]
NDG’s departments disagree about which customer records, stock figures, and financial transactions should be trusted. The IT team discovers that the system contains duplicate customer profiles, inconsistent product codes, incomplete transaction histories, and records that have been manually changed without clear explanations.
At the same time, the organisation is concerned that unauthorised individuals may have accessed confidential information.
Required:
a) Critically examine the difference between data and information. Explain how inaccurate, incomplete, inconsistent, outdated, or duplicated data could lead to poor business decisions at NDG. (6 marks)
b) Propose a practical approach for identifying, correcting, validating, and maintaining the company’s customer, inventory, and financial data. Explain how the organisation should prevent similar data-quality problems during future system changes. (7 marks)
c) Analyse the importance of access control, authentication, authorisation, audit trails, and the principle of least privilege. Explain how these mechanisms could have reduced the likelihood or impact of the incidents described. (6 marks)
d) Management discovers that backups exist but have never been tested. Evaluate the risks associated with relying on unverified backups and recommend a recovery strategy that protects data integrity, business continuity, and the availability of essential systems. (6 marks)
QUESTION 4: CYBER SECURITY GOVERNANCE, RISK MANAGEMENT AND ETHICAL RESPONSIBILITY
[25 MARKS]
The board must decide how to respond to the incidents and prevent further disruption. The managing director wants the IT department to install more security software immediately. The finance manager wants to dismiss the employee associated with the suspicious login. Meanwhile, the IT team argues that the company needs a structured security programme rather than isolated technical purchases.
The organisation has limited resources and cannot address every weakness simultaneously.
Required:
a) Develop a prioritised risk management plan identifying at least five significant risks from the scenario. For each risk, explain its potential impact, likelihood considerations, and proposed treatment. Justify which risks should receive immediate attention and why. (7 marks)
b) Design a practical incident response procedure for NDG, from the initial reporting of a suspected incident through investigation, containment, recovery, and post-incident review. Explain why evidence should be preserved and why employees should not be blamed before the facts are established. (6 marks)
c) Evaluate the responsibilities of senior management, the IT department, system users, and external technology providers in maintaining Information Systems security. Explain why cybersecurity cannot be treated as the IT department’s responsibility alone. (6 marks)
d) Recommend four long-term measures that NDG should adopt to improve Information Systems governance, employee awareness, system reliability, and cybersecurity. Explain how the board could measure whether these measures are working in practice. (6 marks)
END OF EXAMINATION
